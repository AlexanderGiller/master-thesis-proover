\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

This chapter introduces proof certification and the notion of a proof verifier, recalls the parts of first-order logic and Skolemization that the verifier depends on, describes the TPTP formats and explains the setting of ProoVer 2026.

%==============================================================================
\subsection{Automated Theorem Proving and Proof Certification}
\label{subsec:atp-certification}

For first-order logic the dominant automated provers are saturation provers based on resolution \citep{Robinson1965} and superposition, such as Vampire \citep{RiazanovVoronkov2002} and E \citep{SchulzCV2019}.
They combine many techniques such as clause selection heuristics, indexing and splitting, and are therefore very complex.
Vampire in particular has dominated the yearly CASC competition for many years and won all eight divisions of CASC-30 in 2025~\citep{Sut16,CASC30}.
But trusting such a system blindly is difficult, because a single implementation error can lead to a wrong answer without any visible sign.
The formal verification of proofs produced by the prover Leo-III, for example, uncovered several bugs in Leo-III itself \citep{taprogge2026formal}.

Proof verification separates \emph{proof search} from \emph{proof checking}.
The prover produces a proof object, and an independent checker validates each inference step.
The most important property of the checker is \emph{soundness}, which means that it never accepts an invalid proof.
Because the checker is much simpler than the prover, the trusted computing base (TCB) moves from the prover to a much smaller program \citep{Pollack1998,chihani2013foundational}.

This principle goes back to two traditions of interactive theorem proving.
Barendregt and Wiedijk named it the \emph{de Bruijn criterion}: a system satisfies it if its proofs can be checked by a small and simple program that a sceptical user could inspect or even write himself \citep{BarendregtWiedijk2005}.
The \emph{LCF approach} \citep{GordonMilnerWadsworth1979} reaches the same goal by making theorems values of an abstract data type whose only constructors are the primitive rules of the logic. Every theorem is therefore built by a small trusted kernel. The kernel of HOL Light, for example, has only a few hundred lines of code \citep{Harrison2009HOLLight}.

For checking automated proofs, the analogy is not perfect.
An LCF kernel knows a handful of primitive rules, whereas a TSTP derivation consists of coarse steps with many different rule names.
A checker can implement many of these rules itself, which makes it larger than a kernel, or delegate them to an external prover, which brings a full proof search back into the trusted part.
Balancing these two options is the central design question of this thesis.

\subsection{Proof Verifiers}
\label{subsec:verifier-def}

GDV calls itself a \emph{derivation verifier} \citep{Sutcliffe2006GDV}, whereas ProoVer and N\"orgler speak of \emph{proof checkers} \citep{taprogge2026lightweight}. In this thesis both words are used as synonyms.
A \emph{proof verifier} takes a problem and a derivation and returns a verdict.
The problem consists of a set of axioms and a conjecture, and the derivation claims to prove the conjecture from the axioms.
In ProoVer the verdicts are \texttt{VerifiedGood}, which means that the derivation is a correct proof of the conjecture, \texttt{VerifiedBad}, where the derivation is an incorrect proof, and the abstentions \texttt{Unknown} and \texttt{Timeout}.
A derivation is \emph{valid} for a problem if
\begin{enumerate}
  \item its names are unique, every parent exists and its dependency graph is acyclic (\emph{structure}),
  \item every imported axiom and conjecture is a copy of a formula of the problem (\emph{faithfulness}),
  \item every step is correct with respect to its declared status (\emph{logical correctness}, Section~\ref{subsec:proof-objects-soundness}), and
  \item it is a refutation that negates the conjecture and derives \texttt{\$false}.
\end{enumerate}
These groups correspond to the taxonomy of checks of \citet{taprogge2026lightweight} (Table~\ref{tab:check-taxonomy}).

A verifier is \emph{sound} if it returns \texttt{VerifiedGood} only for valid derivations, and \emph{complete} for a class of derivations if it returns \texttt{VerifiedGood} for every valid derivation of that class.
The GDV documentation uses the two words from the point of view of faults. Its ``complete'' (finding every fault) therefore corresponds to sound here \citep{GDVSeminar}.
Whether a step follows from its parents is only semi-decidable in first-order logic. A verifier that asks a prover can therefore not always answer within a time limit.
It therefore needs the abstentions and a policy for using them. This thesis uses a \emph{fail-closed} policy, under which a step or proof that cannot be established is never reported as \texttt{VerifiedGood}.

\subsection{First-Order Logic and Skolemization}
\label{subsec:fol}

Only the notions needed in this thesis are recalled here.
Refer to \citet{Fitting1996} for a full treatment.
Terms are built from variables and function symbols, atoms from predicate symbols applied to terms or from equations, and formulas from atoms, $\top$, $\bot$, the connectives $\neg, \wedge, \vee, \Rightarrow, \Leftrightarrow$ and the quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$.
Two formulas that differ only in the names of bound variables are \emph{alpha-equivalent}, which is the notion of equality the verifier uses to compare formulas.
A set of sentences $\Gamma$ entails $\varphi$ if every model of $\Gamma$ satisfies $\varphi$, and
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma \models \varphi \quad\text{iff}\quad \Gamma \cup \{\neg\varphi\} \text{ is unsatisfiable.}
  \label{eq:refutation}
\end{equation}
Most provers use this \emph{refutation principle} \citep{Robinson1965}. They negate the conjecture, add it to the axioms and derive a contradiction, which is why TSTP proofs end in $\bot$ \citep{SutcliffeSCV2006}.

The \emph{polarity} of a subformula is positive if it lies under an even number of negations, counting the antecedent of an implication as a negation, and negative otherwise \citep{NonnengartWeidenbach2001}.
A quantifier at a negative position behaves like its dual. In $(\exists x\, p(x)) \Rightarrow q$, for example, the existential quantifier is effectively universal.
Below an equivalence a subformula occurs in both polarities.

\paragraph{Normal forms and Skolemization.}
\label{subsec:normal-forms}

Many provers transform their input into clause normal form by eliminating implications and equivalences, pushing negations inward, eliminating existential quantifiers and distributing disjunctions.
To avoid an exponential blow-up, they replace subformulas by fresh predicates with a defining equivalence \citep{Tseitin1983,PlaistedGreenbaum1986,NonnengartWeidenbach2001}.
Such definitions introduce new symbols, and the result is only equisatisfiable with the input.

\emph{Skolemization} eliminates an existential quantifier.
An existential variable $y$ in the scope of the universal variables $x_1,\dots,x_n$ is replaced by a term $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with a fresh \emph{Skolem function} $f$:
\begin{equation}
  \forall x_1 \dots \forall x_n\, \exists y\, \varphi(x_1,\dots,x_n,y)
  \;\leadsto\;
  \forall x_1 \dots \forall x_n\, \varphi(x_1,\dots,x_n,f(x_1,\dots,x_n)).
  \label{eq:skolemization}
\end{equation}
The result is not equivalent to the original formula, but equisatisfiable, which is enough for a refutation.
For checking this has a central consequence.
The Skolemized formula entails the original one, but a Skolem term with too few arguments yields an even stronger formula that still entails the original one and is no longer equisatisfiable.
A Skolemization step can therefore not be verified by an entailment test. An external tool could still check it by testing equisatisfiability instead of entailment, but this is a harder question than entailment, and the ProoVer rules require that Skolemization is checked internally. An internal checker has to validate the shape of the transformation, the dependencies of the Skolem term on the universal variables in scope and the \emph{freshness} of the symbol \citep{NonnengartWeidenbach2001}.

For example, the correct Skolemization of $\forall x\,\exists y\,\mathit{parent}(y,x)$ is $\forall x\,\mathit{parent}(f(x),x)$.
The formula $\forall x\,\mathit{parent}(c,x)$ also entails the axiom, but together with $\forall x\,\neg\mathit{parent}(x,x)$ it yields the contradiction $\mathit{parent}(c,c)$, although the original formulas are satisfiable.
Freshness matters for the same reason. Replacing $y$ in $\exists y\,p(y)$ by a constant $a$ that already occurs in an axiom $\neg p(a)$ yields a contradiction from satisfiable premises.

\subsection{The TPTP Formats}
\label{subsec:tptp-tstp}

The TPTP library is the standard collection of problems for evaluating ATP systems under comparable conditions \citep{Sutcliffe2017}.
It comes with the TSTP library of solutions \citep{TSTPLibrary}, the SZS ontologies for results and online services such as SystemOnTPTP \citep{Sutcliffe2000SOTPTP}.
The language has dialects for untyped first-order formulas (\texttt{fof}), clauses (\texttt{cnf}) and typed and higher-order logic. ProoVer 2026 and this thesis use \texttt{fof}.

\paragraph{The FOF language.}
\label{subsec:fof-syntax}

A TPTP file is a sequence of \emph{annotated formulas} of the form \texttt{fof(Name, Role, Formula, Source, UsefulInfo)} \citep{TPTPSyntaxBNF}.
The roles that occur in the benchmark are listed in Table~\ref{tab:roles-status}.
Variables start with an upper-case letter, all other symbols with a lower-case letter, and Table~\ref{tab:fof-syntax} lists the logical symbols.
A quantified body that is a binary formula needs parentheses, and the associative connectives \texttt{\&} and \texttt{|} must not be mixed without them.

\begin{table}[!htb]

\label{tab:fof-syntax}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
\toprule
Notion & Symbol & TPTP & Example \\
\midrule
negation & $\neg$ & \texttt{\tptptilde} & \texttt{\tptptilde{}p(X)} \\

\paragraph{TSTP derivations.}
\label{subsec:tstp-derivations}

A derivation is written in the same language and uses the source field to record where each formula comes from \citep{SutcliffeSCV2006,TPTPQuickGuideDerivations}.
A \emph{leaf} is either taken from the problem, \texttt{file(FileName, FormulaName)}, or introduced by the prover, \texttt{introduced(Type, \dots)}.
A derived formula carries an inference record \texttt{inference(Rule, UsefulInfo, Parents)}, whose rule name is not standardized.
Its \texttt{UsefulInfo} holds the status of the step and, for Skolemization, the annotations \texttt{new\_symbols(skolem, [sK0])} and \texttt{skolemize(Var, Term)}.
The status is a value of the SZS ontology \citep{Sutcliffe2008SZS,SZSOntologyWeb}, and Table~\ref{tab:roles-status} summarizes the roles and statuses that occur in the benchmark.

\begin{table}[!htb]
\centering
\small
\caption{Roles and statuses in the ProoVer 2026 proofs \citep{TPTPSyntaxBNF,TPTPQuickGuideDerivations}.}
\label{tab:roles-status}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l P{0.68\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Role or status & Meaning \\
\midrule
\texttt{axiom}, \texttt{hypothesis} & An assumption of the problem, imported with a \texttt{file} source. \\
\addlinespace[3pt]
\texttt{conjecture} & The statement to be proved, imported from the problem. \\
\addlinespace[3pt]
\texttt{negated\_conjecture} & The negation of the conjecture, from which the refutation starts. \\
\addlinespace[3pt]
\texttt{plain} & A derived formula without a special role. \\
\midrule
\texttt{thm} (Theorem) & The formula is derived from its parents. Used by most rules. \\
\addlinespace[3pt]
\texttt{cth} (CounterTheorem) & The negation of the formula is derived from its parent, which must be the conjecture. \\
\addlinespace[3pt]
\texttt{esa} (EquiSatisfiable) & The formula is equisatisfiable with its single parent. Used for Skolemization. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

By convention, each Skolemization step eliminates one variable, and every introduced symbol is globally new \citep{TPTPQuickGuideDerivations}.
These conventions make Skolemization checkable at all \citep{taprogge2026lightweight}, but provers do not always follow them (Section~\ref{subsec:disc-transfer}).
Solvers report their result and delimit the proof with SZS lines such as \texttt{\% SZS status Theorem}, and ProoVer reuses this style for its verdicts.

\paragraph{Soundness of refutations.}
\label{subsec:proof-objects-soundness}

Let $\Gamma$ be the axioms and the negated conjecture of a derivation that ends in $\bot$.
A step with status \texttt{thm} is sound if its conclusion is entailed by its premises.
A step with status \texttt{esa} is sound if every model of its premises can be extended, by interpreting the fresh symbols, to a model of the premises and the conclusion.
If all steps are sound and the dependency graph is acyclic, an induction over the graph shows that $\Gamma$ together with all derived formulas is satisfiable whenever $\Gamma$ is.
Since $\bot$ is derived, $\Gamma$ is unsatisfiable, and by Equation~\ref{eq:refutation} the conjecture follows from the axioms.
The argument also shows that the check for a final \texttt{\$false} is part of soundness, because a derivation of sound steps that never reaches $\bot$ proves nothing.

\subsection{The ProoVer 2026 Competition}
\label{subsec:proover2026}

ProoVer is a competition for proof checkers in the TPTP ecosystem, inspired by CASC \citep{proover-competition}.
Participants receive correct and deliberately flawed proofs and must decide for each whether it is valid.
The rules of the 2026 edition fix the following points.
\begin{itemize}
  \item All proofs are refutations that end in \texttt{\$false}.
  \item The import of axioms and conjectures (alpha-equivalent to the referenced formula), \texttt{negated\_conjecture} and \texttt{skolemize} must be checked \emph{internally}.
  \item All other rules may be checked by an external prover, but only for steps with status \texttt{thm} or \texttt{cth}, only with the premises of the step and never by re-proving the whole conjecture.
\end{itemize}
The benchmark was announced with 100 problems, half of them with a valid proof, and a wall-clock limit of 30\,s per problem.
Nine of the 100 pairs are not part of the published results. The scores therefore refer to the remaining 91 pairs~\citep{ProoVer2026Results}.
A correctly rejected bad proof earns $+2$, a correctly accepted good proof $+1$, an abstention $0$, a rejected good proof $-1$ and an accepted bad proof $-10$ \citep{ProoVer2026Proceedings}.
The proofs use a natural-deduction-like vocabulary with rules such as \texttt{instantiate}, \texttt{modus\_ponens}, \texttt{split\_conjunct}, \texttt{skolemize} and the generic \texttt{consequence}, and some instantiation steps carry \texttt{new\_symbols(herbrand, \dots)} annotations.
