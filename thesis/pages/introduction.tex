\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{subsec:motivation}

Automated theorem proving (ATP) is used in many areas, from formal reasoning in mathematics to software verification and knowledge-based systems~\citep{HandbookAR2001}.
The more complex ATP systems become, the more likely they are to contain bugs, and a short answer such as ``the conjecture is a theorem'' is then hard to trust \citep{taprogge2026formal}.
A common solution is to let the prover output a derivation, also called a \emph{proof object}, that justifies its answer step by step, and to check it with a separate and much simpler program, namely a \emph{proof checker} or \emph{proof verifier}.
Trust then no longer rests on a large prover whose behavior becomes harder to follow with every optimization, but on a small checker that is easy to inspect. This idea goes back to the de Bruijn criterion and the LCF approach (Section~\ref{subsec:atp-certification}).

In the TPTP ecosystem~\citep{Sut24}, derivations are exchanged in the TSTP format~\citep{Sut10, Sut07-CSR}.
A TSTP derivation is a directed acyclic graph of formulas in which each node records the rule that produced it and the premises it depends on.
In the TPTP world, the established checker for such derivations is GDV~\citep{Sutcliffe2006GDV,GDVSeminar}. It asks trusted external ATP systems to confirm each step.
This approach works for every inference rule, but it is slow and depends on the external systems.
As a consequence, more recent checkers handle frequent rules internally (Chapter~\ref{sec:related-work}).

The first edition of the ProoVer competition turns this setting into a benchmark~\citep{proover-competition}.
Each checker receives a first-order (FOF) problem and a TSTP proof, some of which contain deliberate errors, and has to classify the proof as valid or invalid, or abstain.
Because a wrongly accepted proof costs ten times as much as a correctly accepted proof gains, a checker that cannot decide a step faces a genuine trade-off. Accepting pays off only if the proof is very likely valid, rejecting only if it is likely invalid, and in between abstaining is the better choice.

\subsection{Research Questions and Scope}
\label{subsec:rq}

The objective of this thesis is to design and implement a Python-based verifier for TSTP derivations that is sound and competitive.
\emph{Sound} means that each accepted step is justified by the semantics of its rule, and \emph{competitive} means a high score under the ProoVer 2026 scoring scheme (Chapter~\ref{sec:requirements}) within the wall-clock limit of 30 seconds per proof.
The scope is limited to FOF problems and the inference rules of the ProoVer 2026 benchmark.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] Which inference rules must a verifier support to cover the ProoVer 2026 benchmark, and which can be left to an external prover?
  \item[RQ2] How can a modular architecture keep the trusted part of the verifier small and easy to extend?
  \item[RQ3] How do correctness, run time and memory consumption behave on the benchmark and on malformed derivations?
\end{description}

\subsection{Contributions and Structure}
\label{subsec:contributions}

The thesis contributes
(i) a fail-closed, three-valued verdict policy derived from the scoring scheme of the competition,
(ii) \TSTPy{}, a modular verifier for TSTP derivations with internal checks for most rules, a specific Skolemization check and a controlled fallback to the prover Vampire, and
(iii) an evaluation on the ProoVer 2026 benchmark, on generated rule-level tests and on mutated proofs.

Chapter~\ref{sec:background} introduces the logical background, the TPTP/TSTP formats and the competition, and Chapter~\ref{sec:related-work} discusses related checkers.
Chapter~\ref{sec:requirements} states the task and the verdict policy, Chapters~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:implementation} present the design and its implementation.
Finally Chapter~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports the experiments, whereas Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the arising implications and limits, while Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the thesis.
