\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates \TSTPy{} against the research questions of Section~\ref{subsec:rq}.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:eval-setup}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
Of the \EvalNPrv{} problem/proof pairs initially provided for ProoVer 2026, \EvalOffN{} (\EvalOffValid{} valid, \EvalOffInvalid{} invalid) form the scored benchmark, which is called the \emph{ProoVer set}. Their expected results come from the file \texttt{results.csv} of the organizers \citep{ProoVer2026Results}.
The remaining \EvalSampleN{} deprecated pairs are only reported where this is stated.
The \emph{TST set} consists of 76 proofs written for this thesis to test individual checks (Table~\ref{tab:eval-tst}, results per proof in Table~\ref{tab:app-tst}), five of which are edge cases with an open expected result and are not counted.
The \emph{Extra set} consists of \EvalExtraN{} generated proofs for the rules that the TST set hardly covers (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-extra} and Table~\ref{tab:app-ext}).

\paragraph{Metrics and prover.}
The primary metric is the score under the ProoVer 2026 scheme with the default three-valued verdict. The verdict matrix, wrong acceptances, run time, memory and the share of steps decided without the prover are also reported.
All delegated steps go to Vampire~\citep{KovacsVoronkov2013} in CASC mode, the strongest first-order prover of recent CASC competitions~\citep{CASC30}.
Because every delegated step needs a confirmation of the prover, the strength of the prover determines how many valid proofs can be verified, which is why the strongest system was chosen instead of comparing several.

\paragraph{Environment.}
All measurements were made on a laptop with \EvalCPU{} (\EvalCores{} threads, \EvalRAM{} RAM) under WSL~2, with Python~\EvalPython{}, \texttt{lark}~\EvalLark{}, \EvalVampire and a limit of 30\,s per proof, on \EvalDate{} with commit \EvalCommit.
Each proof was checked \EvalReps{} times in a fresh process, and run times are medians.
As in the competition, a run includes the start-up of the interpreter and ends with the verdict.

\subsection{Correctness and Score}
\label{subsec:eval-correctness}

Table~\ref{tab:eval-matrix} shows the verdicts on the ProoVer set, and the file \href{https://github.com/AlexanderGiller/master-thesis-proover/blob/main/results/eval/per_proof.csv}{\texttt{results/eval/per\_proof.csv}} of the repository lists the result for every proof (Appendix~\ref{app:per-proof}).
\TSTPy{} accepts all \EvalOffAccValid{} valid proofs, rejects \EvalOffRejInvalid{} of the \EvalOffInvalid{} invalid proofs and answers \emph{unknown} for one, which gives \EvalOffScore{} of \EvalOffMaxScore{} points (\EvalOffScorePct\,\%) without a wrong acceptance.
The two missing points stem from \texttt{PRV097}, which contains a step with the rule \texttt{trivial} and status \texttt{esa}.
There is no internal checker for it, and the step must not be delegated, because the status \texttt{esa} does not allow \textbf{it} The verifier therefore abstains (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-errors}).
With the binary verdict this proof is rejected, and the score is the maximum of \EvalOffScoreBinary{} points.
On the deprecated proofs, too, all verdicts agree with the expected results.
Two invalid proofs, \texttt{PRV091} and the deprecated \texttt{PRV057}, are rejected by the parser, because they use names starting with an upper-case letter as a Skolem symbol or a step name. Such names are variables in TPTP. The proofs are therefore not well-formed, which is their intended defect.

The exception, \texttt{TST043}, derives \texttt{\$false} from the axioms and the negated conjecture in a single step.

\paragraph{Rule-level test suite.}
\label{subsec:eval-extra}

The TST set concentrates on provenance, the negated conjecture and Skolemization, where competition proofs are most likely to contain errors.
For the other rules the Extra set was generated. For each of \EvalExtraRules{} rules, it contains a proof that applies the rule correctly and several variants with a specific defect, such as a wrong premise, an inverted conclusion, a missing side condition, or a captured variable, plus \EvalExtraEdge{} sound but unusual edge cases.
The verifier accepts all \EvalExtraAccOk{} valid proofs and rejects all \EvalExtraRejOk{} invalid ones (Table~\ref{tab:app-ext}), and in every case the rule checker itself reports the error, not only the final \texttt{\$false} step.
Of the edge cases, \EvalExtraEdgeAcc{} are accepted and \EvalExtraEdgeRej{} rejected, which was regarded as cautious behavior on formulations that no competition proof uses.
In earlier versions, this suite exposed defects that the ProoVer set could not reveal, because it contains no proof that exercises them.

\subsection{Rule Coverage and Modularity (RQ1, RQ2)}
\label{subsec:eval-coverage}

The ProoVer set contains \EvalDerivedSteps{} derived steps of \EvalRulesInSet{} rules.\footnote{\texttt{PRV091} is not counted, because the parser rejects it.}
Of these steps, \EvalInternalSteps{} (\EvalInternalPct\,\%) are decided by an internal checker, \EvalAtpOnlySteps{} by the prover alone and \EvalFallbackSteps{} by the prover after a failed shape check.
The aggregate is dominated by three proofs with 10\,005 steps each that consist almost entirely of \texttt{modus\_ponens} and \texttt{skolemize}. In half of the proofs, at least \EvalInternalMedianShare\,\% of the steps are decided internally.

Figure~\ref{fig:eval-rule-coverage} shows the rules by the number of proofs that use them.
\texttt{consequence} is the most frequent rule (87 of \EvalOffN{} proofs), followed by \texttt{skolemize} (41) and \texttt{instantiate} (29).
Apart from \texttt{consequence}, which has no syntactic criterion, every rule that occurs in more than five proofs has an internal checker.
Even for \texttt{consequence}, \EvalConsShortcut{} of the \EvalConsSteps{} steps have complementary parents and are decided by the shortcut of Section~\ref{subsec:design-atp} without a prover call.
Of the \EvalRulesDelegated{} rules without one, only \texttt{consequence} and \texttt{add\_hypothesis} occur in more than four proofs, and most of the others in a single one.
In Figure~\ref{fig:eval-rule-coverage} these single-proof rules are grouped. \EvalSingleRulesInternal{} are checked internally, and \EvalSingleRulesProver{} are decided by the prover.
In answer to RQ1, a verifier that covers Skolemization, instantiation, generalization, the propositional rules and the structural rules checks the bulk of the benchmark itself, and delegation handles the long tail of rules, for which an own checker would add trusted code for a handful of steps.

\subsection{Robustness (RQ3)}
\label{subsec:eval-robustness}

\paragraph{Mutated proofs.}
To generate mutated proofs, derived steps of \EvalMutProofs{} valid proofs with at most 80 steps were mutated by changing a connective, swapping the sides of an implication, swapping a quantifier, negating an atom, removing or replacing a premise, or changing the status \texttt{thm} to \texttt{cth}, at most two steps per proof and operator (Table~\ref{tab:eval-mutations}).
A mutant that the verifier accepts is not necessarily a miss, because many mutations preserve validity. Every accepted mutant was therefore checked with Vampire.
Of the \EvalMutN{} mutants, \EvalMutKilled{} are rejected, and all \EvalMutEq{} accepted ones are still valid. Every mutant that is no longer valid is thus detected.
Most accepted mutants (41 of \EvalMutEq{}) change the status to \texttt{cth} of a step that still follows from its premises, which the verifier correctly treats as informational. The others swap the operands of a symmetric connective or cite another premise from which the conclusion also follows.
Because this classification only tests entailment, it cannot judge defects such as a dropped Skolem dependency, which the TST group \texttt{sk} covers instead.

\paragraph{Missing prover.}
Running the ProoVer set with a prover command that does not exist (Table~\ref{tab:eval-noatp}) accepts no invalid proof, which confirms the fail-closed policy.
Although \texttt{consequence} occurs in 87 proofs (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-rule-coverage}), only \EvalNoAtpUncertain{} proofs become \emph{Unknown}, for two reasons.
First, \EvalNoAtpVerifiedValid{} of the \EvalOffValid{} valid proofs need no prover at all, because their final step has complementary parents and is decided by the shortcut, or because they end with an internally checked resolution step. The other \EvalNoAtpUncertainValid{} valid proofs become \emph{Unknown}.
Second, an invalid proof is rejected as soon as any check finds a defect, whatever happens to its other steps. For \EvalNoAtpRejInvalid{} of the \EvalOffInvalid{} invalid proofs the defect lies in an internally checked step, and only \EvalNoAtpUncertainInvalid{} have their defect in a step that needs the prover.
With the binary verdict the \EvalNoAtpRejValidBinary{} undecided valid proofs are rejected instead, which costs one point each, whereas the three-valued verdict abstains on them.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{subsec:eval-ablation}

\paragraph{Internal checkers against the prover.}
To see what the internal checkers add, up to eight internally checked steps per proof of the ProoVer set (proofs up to 400 steps) were sampled and Vampire was asked with a limit of 10\,s whether they follow from their premises.
Of the \EvalIntSteps{} steps, the prover confirms \EvalIntAccYes{} that the checker accepts, and it refutes none of them.
For \EvalIntUnknown{} accepted steps, a \texttt{rename\_variable} step in \texttt{PRV008}, a \texttt{conjunction} step in \texttt{PRV012} and an \texttt{instantiate\_mp} step in \texttt{PRV018}, Vampire does not finish within 10\,s, although the steps are trivial for the internal checker.
On the other hand, it confirms \EvalIntRejYes{} steps that the checker rejects, a \texttt{modus\_ponens} step in \texttt{PRV023} and a \texttt{rewrite} step in \texttt{PRV024}. Their conclusions follow from the premises, but not by the cited rule.
The internal checks are therefore faster on hard-looking trivial steps and stricter than entailment, which is intended, because the task is to verify the stated inference.

\paragraph{Three-valued verdict.}
Table~\ref{tab:eval-noatp} also compares the two verdict policies.
With the prover, the binary verdict scores two points more, because it rejects \texttt{PRV097} instead of abstaining.
Without the prover the order is reversed, \EvalNoAtpScoreTernary{} against \EvalNoAtpScoreBinary{} points, because \EvalNoAtpUncertain{} proofs are undecided and a rejected valid proof costs a point, whereas an abstention costs nothing.
The three-valued verdict was chosen as the default, because it limits the loss when the prover is missing or slow and distinguishes incorrect steps from steps that could not be checked.

\subsection{Remaining Errors and Threats to Validity}
\label{subsec:eval-errors}

The only suboptimal verdict is the abstention on \texttt{PRV097}.
A checker for \texttt{trivial} with status \texttt{esa} would turn it into a verdict, but the benchmark contains only one such step, which is not enough to derive the intended semantics with confidence, and a guess could cause a wrong acceptance.

\texttt{PRV099} is the only proof whose run time is dominated by the prover.
Its step \texttt{hard} derives \texttt{\$false} from the negated conjecture alone, which is the pigeonhole formula for ten pigeons and nine holes with 90 variables and 415 short clauses.
Every resolution refutation of this formula has exponential size \citep{Haken1985}. Vampire therefore needs 9.0\,s although the formula is small.
After it, the proof contains a second step \texttt{badcheap} that derives \texttt{\$false} from the satisfiable axiom $g(e)$ and is refuted in less than 0.1\,s.
The proof is presumably designed to test whether a checker spends its time on the hard step before it reaches the cheap defect.
The verifier returns the correct verdict, but it falls into this trap, because it checks the steps in file order and calls the prover for \texttt{hard} before it reaches \texttt{badcheap}.
Checking cheap steps first or running prover calls in parallel with a short first limit would avoid this and is left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
In an earlier run on a two-core virtual machine with Vampire~5.1.0, the call for \texttt{hard} exceeded the limit and the proof counted as an abstention, which would have cost two points.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
\label{subsec:eval-threats}

The verifier was developed against the ProoVer sample proofs. The ProoVer set is therefore not an independent test set, and the TST and Extra sets reflect the author's understanding of the rules.
The mutation and missing-prover experiments do not depend on the benchmark, but they rely on Vampire to classify accepted mutants. This classification is only as reliable as the prover.
The benchmark comes from a fixed set of generators, and the results do not transfer to other provers (Section~\ref{subsec:disc-transfer}).
The competition runs each checker on one of two Intel Xeon E5-2620 v4 CPUs with 2.1\,GHz and 128\,GiB memory.
A single core of this server processor is slower than a core of the laptop used here, and the verifier uses only one core.
Assuming a factor of 1.5 to 2.5, which is an estimate and not a measurement, the median would rise to two to four seconds and the large proofs to 6 to 11\,s, while \texttt{PRV099} could reach 16 to 28\,s and come close to the limit.
A timeout there would turn the rejection into an abstention, but could not lead to a wrong acceptance.
