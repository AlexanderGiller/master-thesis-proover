\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates the verifier against the research questions of Section~\ref{subsec:rq}.
Section~\ref{subsec:eval-setup} describes the benchmarks, the metrics, the external prover, and the experimental setup.
Section~\ref{subsec:eval-correctness} reports correctness and score, Section~\ref{subsec:eval-extra} the results of the rule-level test suite, and Section~\ref{subsec:eval-coverage} the coverage of the inference rules (RQ1).
Section~\ref{subsec:eval-modularity} reports the properties of the architecture (RQ2), Sections~\ref{subsec:eval-runtime} to~\ref{subsec:eval-ablation} run time, robustness, and ablations (RQ3), and Sections~\ref{subsec:eval-errors} and~\ref{subsec:eval-threats} analyze the remaining errors and the threats to validity.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:eval-setup}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
The evaluation uses three proof sets.
Of the \EvalNPrv{} problem/proof pairs that were initially provided for ProoVer 2026, only \EvalOffN{} pairs (directory \texttt{PRV}, \EvalOffValid{} valid, \EvalOffInvalid{} invalid) form the actual benchmark. These \EvalOffN{} pairs are called the \emph{ProoVer set} in this chapter, and all statistics below refer to them.
The remaining \EvalSampleN{} pairs (directory \texttt{Deprecated}) no longer appear in the results published by the organizers and were apparently not scored in the competition, whose scores refer to the \EvalOffN{} pairs.
Of the \EvalSampleN{} deprecated pairs, the expected results are only known from the version of the benchmark distributed before the results.
A verdict on them therefore cannot be scored reliably. They only appear where this is stated explicitly, namely in the last row of the verdict matrix (Table~\ref{tab:eval-matrix}), in the comparison of versions on all proofs (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-extra}), and as examples (\texttt{PRV005}, \texttt{PRV057}).
The expected results of the \EvalOffN{} pairs are taken from the file \texttt{results.csv} that the organizers provide with the benchmark \citep{ProoVer2026Proceedings}, those of the deprecated pairs from the earlier version of the benchmark.
The \emph{TST set} (directory \texttt{Test}) consists of 76 proofs written for this thesis to test individual checks (Table~\ref{tab:eval-tst}).
Each has an expected result of \emph{correct} or \emph{incorrect}. Five are \emph{edge cases} whose expected result is open (for example two negated conjectures, or a quantifier block in a different order), and are excluded from the agreement counts.
The \emph{Extra set} (directory \texttt{Extra}) consists of \EvalExtraN{} proofs that were generated for the rules that the TST set covers only marginally (resolution, paramodulation, reflexivity, transitivity, and the propositional rules).
It is described in Section~\ref{subsec:eval-extra}.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
The primary metric is the total score under the ProoVer 2026 scheme (Section~\ref{subsec:req-scoring}): $+1$ for a correctly accepted valid proof, $+2$ for a correctly rejected invalid proof, $0$ for abstention, $-1$ for a wrongly rejected valid proof, and $-10$ for a wrongly accepted invalid proof.
The verifier reports one of the three verdicts \emph{verified}, \emph{failed}, and \emph{unknown} (SZS: \texttt{VerifiedGood}, \texttt{VerifiedBad}, \texttt{Unknown}). A proof is \emph{unknown} if the only findings are steps that could not be decided (no checker for the rule, or no answer of the prover).
Unknown and a time-out both score $0$ points.
Unless stated otherwise, the numbers refer to this default policy. The option \texttt{--binary-verdict} maps unknown to failed (the \emph{binary policy}), and Section~\ref{subsec:eval-ablation} compares the two.
In addition we report the verdict matrix, the wrong acceptances separately, the run time per proof, the share of steps that are decided without the external prover, and the peak memory.

\paragraph{External prover.}
All steps that no internal checker decides are delegated to Vampire~\citep{KovacsVoronkov2013} in its CASC mode, and we use no other prover.
The reason is that Vampire is the reference system for this task.
It has been among the strongest systems of the CASC competitions for many years~\citep{Sut16} and won all eight divisions of CASC-30 in 2025~\citep{CASC30}, so that it is generally regarded as the state of the art in automated theorem proving for first-order logic.
For a verifier that needs a confirmation of the prover for every delegated step (a step that Vampire cannot prove stays undecided and prevents acceptance), the strength of the prover directly determines how many valid proofs can be verified, which is why we chose the strongest available system instead of comparing several.
The verifier addresses it through one command line (variables \texttt{EXTERNAL\_ATP\_BINARY} and \texttt{EXTERNAL\_ATP\_COMMAND}) and reads only the SZS status, so that another prover could be used with minimal changes, but this is not evaluated here.
We used \EvalVampire.

\paragraph{Environment.}
All measurements were made on a laptop with \EvalCPU{} (\EvalCores{} threads, \EvalRAM{} RAM available to the virtual machine) under \EvalOS{} (WSL~2 on Windows), with Python~\EvalPython{} and \texttt{lark}~\EvalLark{}, with the time limit of 30\,s per proof.
Each proof was checked in a fresh worker process and run \EvalReps{} times. Run times are medians of these repetitions.
Every run includes the start-up of the interpreter and the import of the verifier (median \EvalInterpStart), because the competition measures the whole process. The time of a run ends with the verdict of the proof, as in the competition. The breakdown into phases in Table~\ref{tab:eval-slowest} uses a separate measurement of the parse time after the check, which does not count towards the total, and all phases of a proof come from the same repetition.
The measurements were made on \EvalDate{} with commit \EvalCommit.
All numbers in this chapter are produced by \texttt{scripts/eval\_all.py}, which writes the tables and macros used here into \texttt{thesis/generated}.

\subsection{Rule-Level Test Suite}
\label{subsec:eval-extra}

The TST set concentrates on provenance, the negated conjecture, and Skolemization, the points on which the competition proofs are most likely to contain errors.
It therefore says little about the checkers of the other rules.
To test them, we generated the Extra set with \texttt{scripts/gen\_extra\_tests.py}. For each of \EvalExtraRules{} rules, the generator produces a small problem and a proof that applies the rule correctly, and several variants with a specific defect (a wrong premise, an inverted conclusion, a missing side condition, a capture of a variable).
Overall the set contains \EvalExtraValid{} valid proofs, \EvalExtraInvalid{} invalid proofs, and \EvalExtraEdge{} edge cases, that is, proofs that are sound but unusual (for example a reflexivity step in an unusual orientation).
Each case is a proof together with its problem file, and the set is run through the same batch mode of \texttt{main.py} as the other sets (\texttt{--dir ProoVer2026/Extra}).
Of the \EvalExtRunsN{} cases, \EvalExtRunsAgree{} agree with the expectation (the edge cases not counted), there is no wrong acceptance and no wrong rejection, and the score is \EvalExtRunsScore{} points. The median run time is \EvalExtRunsWallMedian.

Table~\ref{tab:eval-extra} shows that the verifier accepts all \EvalExtraAccOk{} valid proofs and rejects all \EvalExtraRejOk{} invalid proofs, and that in every case the rejection comes from the check of the rule itself, not only from the final \texttt{\$false} step: the rule checker reports an error for \EvalExtraByRule{} of the \EvalExtraRejOk{} invalid proofs.
Of the \EvalExtraEdge{} edge cases, \EvalExtraEdgeAcc{} are accepted and \EvalExtraEdgeRej{} are rejected. We regard the rejections as cautious behavior, because they concern formulations that no competition prover produces.

The result above is the state after three corrections that the first run of the Extra set made necessary.
Before them, the verifier accepted an invalid proof in which a doubly negated conjecture was ``negated'' by adding a negation, accepted a variable capture by \texttt{instantiate}, and accepted a resolution step whose premises shared a variable name or whose unifier was circular.
We describe these defects and their corrections in Section~\ref{subsec:impl-bugs}.
The corrections do not change any verdict on the ProoVer set, which we confirmed by re-running it with and without them.
This also shows a limitation. The ProoVer set could not detect these defects, because it contains no proof that exercises them.
The same holds for the defects found later, the polarity of a quantifier in the antecedent of an implication and the unchecked role \texttt{hypothesis} (Section~\ref{subsec:impl-bugs}).
We compared the old and the corrected version on all 285 proofs of the four directories. The corrected Skolem check additionally rejects the step \texttt{s1} of \texttt{TST064}, a proof that was already rejected, and the corrected provenance check rejects \texttt{TST072}, which was wrongly accepted before. No other finding and no verdict on the ProoVer and Extra sets changes.

The two views differ considerably.
Counted by steps, \texttt{modus\_ponens} and \texttt{skolemize} dominate, because the three large proofs consist almost entirely of them.
Counted by proofs, \texttt{consequence} is the most frequent rule (87 of \EvalOffN{} proofs), followed by \texttt{skolemize} (41) and \texttt{instantiate} (29), and apart from \texttt{consequence}, which has no syntactic criterion, every rule that occurs in more than five proofs has an internal checker.
Of the \EvalRulesDelegated{} rules without an internal checker, only \texttt{consequence} and \texttt{add\_hypothesis} occur in more than four proofs. The others occur in at most four proofs, most of them in a single one.
This long tail is where delegation pays off. A checker for each of these rules would add code, and therefore trusted code, for a handful of steps, whereas the prover decides them with the same mechanism as \texttt{consequence}.

The aggregate share is dominated by three proofs (\texttt{PRV071}--\texttt{PRV073}) with 10\,005 steps each, which consist almost entirely of \texttt{modus\_ponens} and \texttt{skolemize} steps.
We therefore also give the median over proofs. In half of the proofs, at least \EvalInternalMedianShare\,\% of the steps are decided internally, and the remaining steps are almost always the final \texttt{consequence} step (it occurs in 87 proofs) and \texttt{add\_hypothesis}.
This is intended, because \texttt{consequence} is a generic ``follows from'' step for which no syntactic criterion exists.
Besides these, 12 further rules (among them \texttt{horn}, \texttt{de\_morgan}, \texttt{contrapositive}, \texttt{disjunctive\_syllogism}, and \texttt{over\_generalize}) have no internal checker and are decided by the prover. Most of them occur in a single proof.
In answer to RQ1, a verifier that covers \texttt{skolemize}, \texttt{instantiate}, \texttt{existential\_gen}, \texttt{modus\_ponens}, \texttt{split\_conjunct}, \texttt{conjunction}, and the structural rules checks the bulk of the benchmark itself, and delegation to a prover handles the long tail of rules.

\subsection{Robustness (RQ3)}
\label{subsec:eval-robustness}

Robustness is evaluated in three ways.

\paragraph{Mutation test.}
We generate variants of the valid proofs by applying one mutation each to a derived step and run the verifier on the variant (Table~\ref{tab:eval-mutations}).
The operators change a connective, swap the sides of an implication, swap a quantifier, negate an atom, delete a premise, cite a different earlier step as premise, or change the status \texttt{thm} to \texttt{cth}.
Per proof and operator, up to two applicable steps were mutated, in \EvalMutProofs{} valid proofs with at most 80 steps. This gives \EvalMutN{} mutants.
A mutant that the verifier accepts is not necessarily a miss, because many mutations preserve validity (swapping the operands of a symmetric connective, for example).
We therefore check each surviving mutant with Vampire. If the mutated step still follows from its premises, it is counted as ``still valid'', and otherwise as a real miss.
Of the \EvalMutN{} mutants, \EvalMutKilled{} are rejected, \EvalMutEq{} survive and are still valid, and \EvalMutMiss{} survive and are not valid. The kill rate on the mutants that are no longer valid is \EvalMutRate\,\%.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-mutations} shows the outcome per operator.
The operators that change the formula itself (connective, implication, quantifier, atom) are killed almost completely, and their few survivors are mutations that preserve validity, such as swapping the operands of a symmetric connective or negating an atom of a tautology.
Removing or redirecting a premise is also detected in most cases. The survivors of redirection are steps whose conclusion also follows from the other premise, typically because the proof contains two similar formulas.
No bar has a part for accepted mutants that are no longer valid.
The majority of the survivors (41 of \EvalMutEq{}) come from the status change. The verifier ignores the status \texttt{cth} of a step if the step is entailed by its premises, which is correct for the competition, where status is informational.
Two limits apply.
The oracle checks entailment but not the side conditions of rules, so it is blind to defects such as a dropped Skolem dependency. The mutation experiment therefore does not substitute for the TST group \texttt{sk}.
In addition, the operators are simple, so the result shows that obvious local errors are not missed, not that all errors are detected.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{subsec:eval-ablation}

\paragraph{Internal checkers against the prover.}
The design checks syntactically first and delegates only when necessary (Table~\ref{tab:design-decisions}).
To see what the internal checkers add, we compare them with the prover step by step.
From the valid and invalid proofs of the ProoVer set (proofs up to 400 steps), we sampled up to eight steps per proof, for rules with an internal checker, and asked Vampire whether the step follows from its premises (Table~\ref{tab:eval-internal}).
Of the \EvalIntSteps{} steps, \EvalIntAccYes{} are accepted by the checker and confirmed by the prover, so the two agree.
In \EvalIntUnknown{} cases the prover does not confirm a step that the checker accepts, and in all of them the answer is a timeout after 10\,s: a \texttt{rename\_variable} step in \texttt{PRV008}, a \texttt{conjunction} step in \texttt{PRV012}, and an \texttt{instantiate\_mp} step in \texttt{PRV018}.
The steps are trivial for the internal checker, but Vampire needs more than 10\,s for them in this form.
None of the steps that the checker accepts is refuted by the prover with a counter-model.
This is a second reason for the internal checkers. A verifier that relies on the prover alone would not verify these proofs within a short per-call limit.
In two cases the checker rejects a step that the prover accepts (a \texttt{modus\_ponens} step in \texttt{PRV023} and a \texttt{rewrite} step in \texttt{PRV024}, both in invalid proofs). In both cases the conclusion follows from the premises, but not by the rule that the step cites.
The internal checkers are therefore stricter than entailment, which is intended, because the task is to verify the stated inference and not only that the conclusion is true.
A prover alone could not make this distinction.
The cost of a prover call is moderate in our data (median \EvalIntAtpMedian), so run time is not the main argument for the internal checkers. The arguments are precision, independence from the prover, and robustness against steps that are hard for it.

We did not run a configuration in which \emph{all} steps are sent to the prover, because Skolemization steps, which are not logical consequences, cannot be checked by entailment at all, and because the single-step comparison already answers the question of what the internal checks add.

\paragraph{Three-valued verdict.}
The default policy answers \emph{unknown} for a proof whose only findings are undecided steps and scores it with $0$ points, whereas the binary policy maps it to failed (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-setup}).
To quantify the difference, we score both policies on the same runs (Table~\ref{tab:eval-noatp}).
On the \EvalOffN{} official pairs with the prover, the score is \EvalOffScore{} for the default policy and \EvalOffScoreBinary{} for the binary policy. The default policy is two points lower because \texttt{PRV097} is an abstention instead of a correct rejection (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-correctness}).
Without the prover, the order is reversed, with \EvalNoAtpScoreTernary{} points under the default policy against \EvalNoAtpScoreBinary{} under the binary policy, since many proofs are unknown (\EvalNoAtpUncertain{} of \EvalOffN{}), and a wrongly rejected valid proof costs one point each, whereas an abstention costs nothing.
Abstaining is therefore worthwhile when a missing answer is likely for valid proofs, which is the case when the prover is unavailable or too slow, and it costs points when the undecided step is in an invalid proof, as with \texttt{PRV097}.
We keep the three-valued verdict as the default because it is the more honest report (``this step is wrong'' against ``this step could not be checked''), it limits the loss if the prover behaves differently on the competition machine, and it never turns an unverifiable step into an acceptance. The binary policy is available for a setting in which every invalid proof must be rejected.

\subsection{Error Analysis}
\label{subsec:eval-errors}

\EvalSuboptimal{} of the \EvalOffN{} proofs of the ProoVer set ends with a verdict that is not the best possible one (Table~\ref{tab:eval-errors}): the invalid proof \texttt{PRV097}, whose verdict is unknown instead of failed, as described in Section~\ref{subsec:eval-correctness}.
The step \texttt{trivial} with status \texttt{esa} is a gap in the rule coverage and not an error of the checks. A rule that accepts the step would turn the unknown into a verdict, but we cannot decide from one example which semantics the organizers intend, and a guess could cause a wrong acceptance.
In one case, however, the result depends on the speed of the prover. \texttt{PRV099} contains, after the hard step, a second step \texttt{badcheap} that derives \texttt{\$false} from the satisfiable axiom $g(e)$ and is refuted by Vampire in less than 0.1\,s. The proof is apparently designed to test whether a checker spends its time budget on the hard step before it reaches the cheap defect.
In an earlier run on a two-core virtual machine with Vampire~5.1.0, the proof \texttt{PRV099} exceeded the time limit of 30\,s. The single call for the step \texttt{hard} used the whole remaining budget, and the proof counted as an abstention, which would have cost two points.
On the machine of this chapter, with Vampire~5.0.1, the same call takes 9.0\,s.
Because a single prover call is allowed to use the whole time limit, one hard step can consume the budget of the proof.
A shorter limit per prover call (for example 10\,s) would leave time for the other steps, so that \texttt{PRV099} would still be rejected because of its invalid step \texttt{badcheap}. On the other hand, a valid step that needs more time than this limit would stay undecided, and a valid proof containing it would become unknown. Which choice is better depends on the hardware of the competition, which is discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:eval-threats}.
The longest prover call outside \texttt{PRV099} takes less than 0.3\,s.

The parser errors (\texttt{PRV091} and the deprecated \texttt{PRV057}) are counted as correct, because the proofs are invalid and the verifier answers failed.
In both cases the parse error is the intended defect and not a weakness of the grammar. In TPTP, a word that starts with an upper-case letter is a variable, so that a step name or a function symbol must start with a lower-case letter or be quoted.
\texttt{PRV057} cites a premise \texttt{S0}, which is a variable and not a step name (and no such step exists), and \texttt{PRV091} introduces the Skolem symbol \texttt{SK0}, which is a variable and not a function symbol.
Both proofs are therefore not well-formed TSTP, and rejecting them is the correct verdict for a reason that the specification gives.
Accepting these names would not make a valid proof verifiable, but it would risk accepting the deliberately malformed ones, so the grammar stays strict.
The verdict is, however, given by the parser and not by a check of the proof. The diagnostic ``unexpected token'' does not tell the user which defect was intended.

There is no wrong rejection of a valid proof in the ProoVer set.
The accepted TST proof (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-correctness}), \texttt{TST043}, shows that the verifier does not enforce a granularity of steps, because each step is a valid consequence. Whether this is acceptable depends on the competition rules.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{subsec:eval-threats}

\paragraph{Internal validity.}
The verifier was developed against the ProoVer 2026 sample proofs, so the ProoVer set is not an independent test set. The TST and Extra sets were written by the author and reflect the author's understanding of the rules.
The Extra set found three defects that the other sets did not, which shows that the score on the ProoVer set alone is not sufficient evidence of correctness.
The expected results of the ProoVer set come from the organizers and are not verified independently. The competition itself leaves some edge cases open.
We mitigate this by reporting the mutation experiments, which do not depend on the benchmark, and by reporting wrong acceptances separately.
The mutation oracle is itself an entailment check by Vampire, so its verdicts on the survivors are only as reliable as the prover. A timeout of the oracle is counted as a miss, not as a valid mutant, so the oracle errs on the cautious side.

\paragraph{External validity.}
The benchmark contains FOF proofs from a fixed set of generators. The results do not transfer to proofs from other provers with different rule vocabularies or to other logics.
The share of steps checked internally (\EvalInternalPct\,\%) is mainly a property of the benchmark generators, as a few proofs with many steps dominate it. The median per proof is \EvalInternalMedianShare\,\%.
The scoring scheme and the 30\,s limit are those of one competition.

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
The correctness of the accepted proofs relies on the correctness of the rule checkers, which is supported by tests but not proved, and on the soundness of Vampire.
Run times depend on the hardware and on WSL~2, where start-up is slow. The relations, namely that the prover takes a small part of the time for typical proofs while parsing dominates for the large proofs, are less sensitive to this than the absolute values.
The verdict of the prover can differ between versions and machines when a step is close to the time limit, as the example of \texttt{PRV099} shows. The evaluation uses \EvalVampire.

\paragraph{Hardware of the competition.}
The competition runs each checker on one of two octa-core Intel Xeon E5-2620 v4 CPUs (2.1\,GHz, 16 threads per CPU, 128\,GiB memory, CentOS~7) and imposes no limit on CPU time \citep{proover-competition}.
Compared with the laptop used in this chapter, this machine has more cores and much more memory, but a single core is considerably slower. The Xeon is a server processor from 2016 with a maximum clock rate of about 3\,GHz, whereas the laptop processor reaches up to 4.7\,GHz and belongs to a much newer generation.
The verifier runs on a single core and needs at most \EvalRssMax\,MB of memory, so for its run time only the speed of one core matters.
If we assume that a core of the competition machine is between 1.5 and 2.5 times slower, which is a rough estimate and not a measurement, the median of \EvalWallMedian{} rises to about two to three seconds, which is still far below the limit of 30\,s. The three proofs with 10\,005 steps would need about 6 to 11\,s.
The only proof that could come close to the limit is \texttt{PRV099}, whose prover call for the step \texttt{hard} takes 9.0\,s here, so that the whole proof would need about 16 to 28\,s. If it exceeded the limit, the proof would count as an abstention and lose the two points of a correct rejection, but it could not turn into a wrong acceptance.
The additional cores of the competition machine are not used by the verifier. Running Vampire on several cores or running independent prover calls in parallel could make use of them (Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:eval-summary}

On the \EvalOffN{} proofs of the benchmark, the verifier reaches \EvalOffScore{} of \EvalOffMaxScore{} points under the default three-valued policy (\EvalScore{} of \EvalMaxScore{} including the deprecated proofs, \EvalOffScoreBinary{} and \EvalScoreBinary{} with the binary policy), without a wrong acceptance (RQ1: \EvalRulesInSet{} rules occur, \EvalInternalPct\,\% of the derived steps are checked without the prover, and the rest are mostly the final \texttt{consequence} step).
The architecture keeps the checkers small and separately testable (RQ2): \EvalCheckerLines{} lines in the checker package, \EvalCheckerCov\,\% statement coverage, and a rule-level test suite of \EvalExtraN{} proofs, on which the verifier has no wrong verdict.
The median time per proof is \EvalWallMedian{} and the peak memory \EvalRssMax\,MB at most. The prover is called \EvalAtpCalls{} times and takes \EvalAtpShare\,\% of the time (RQ3).
Without the prover, no invalid proof is accepted, but only half of the valid proofs is verified. Against mutations of valid proofs, every mutant that is no longer valid is rejected.
The open points are the sensitivity of \texttt{PRV099} to the speed of the prover and the rule \texttt{trivial} with status \texttt{esa}, which makes \texttt{PRV097} unknown.
