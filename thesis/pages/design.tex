\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

This chapter presents the architecture of \TSTPy{} independently of its implementation, which follows in Chapter~\ref{sec:implementation}.

\subsection{Principles and Architecture}
\label{subsec:design-principles}

Four principles follow from the requirements of Chapter~\ref{sec:requirements}.
\begin{description}
  \item[Fail closed.] Acceptance is the only outcome that needs positive evidence. Errors and missing tools lead to rejection or abstention, never to acceptance. A step with an unknown rule is accepted only if the prover confirms it.
  \item[Rule locality.] Each rule is checked by a separate function that only sees the step and its parents and returns a list of issues.
  \item[Cheap checks first.] Syntactic checks run before any external tool, and the ATP is only a fallback for steps that cannot be validated syntactically.
  \item[Diagnostics as data.] Checkers return structured issues instead of printing them. Reports, summaries and tests thus use the same information. Appendix~\ref{app:reports} shows an example report.
\end{description}
The benchmark uses more than thirty rule names without a reduction to primitive rules. An LCF-style kernel is therefore not an option.
Instead, all rule checkers rely on the same few operations, namely alpha-equivalence, substitution, template matching and unification. These act as a kernel in a weak sense, because a defect in them affects every rule, whereas a defect in one rule checker stays local.

\paragraph{Architecture.}
\label{subsec:design-overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the pipeline.
A proof is checked in a separate worker process with a wall-clock limit.
The parser turns the problem and the proof into a proof model, global checks validate the proof as a whole and a dispatcher passes each step to the checker of its rule or, if there is none, to the ATP.
The verdict aggregation finally combines all findings into one verdict.

\subsection{Proof Model and Global Checks}
\label{subsec:design-model}
\label{subsec:design-global}

An \emph{annotated formula} has a name, a role, a formula and a justification, which is either a source in the problem file or an \emph{inference record} with the rule name, the parent names, the status and the symbol annotations.
Formulas are immutable syntax trees in which conjunctions and disjunctions are n-ary, which makes flattening explicit and simplifies the junction and clausal checks.
Roles, statuses and rule names are enumerations. Unknown values are thus detected instead of silently ignored.

Four checks concern the proof as a whole.
\emph{Provenance} compares every formula imported with a \texttt{file(\dots)} source with the cited problem formula up to alpha-equivalence and compares the roles.
The \emph{dependency check} rejects cycles and any use of the conjecture except by its negation, because otherwise the proof would assume what it has to refute.
\emph{Unique names} rejects a proof in which a name is defined twice, and \emph{termination} requires a derived step with the formula \texttt{\$false}.
Termination does not require that this step depends on the negated conjecture. A problem with contradictory axioms is therefore not rejected.

\subsection{Step Dispatch and Rule Checkers}
\label{subsec:design-dispatch}

\begin{figure}[!b]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.38\textheight,keepaspectratio]{diagrams/dag_prv089.pdf}
\caption{Dependency graph of the valid benchmark proof \texttt{PRV089}. Each edge leads from a parent to the step derived from it, and the color shows which check validates the node.}
\label{fig:design-dag}
\end{figure}

All step checkers have the same interface. They take a step and its parents and return a list of issues, each with the step name and a reason.
Before calling a checker, the dispatcher checks what is common to all rules, namely that every parent exists, that the number of parents fits the rule and that the status is the one the rule requires.
A checker never throws an exception because a proof is invalid, and an exception therefore indicates an input the verifier cannot process.
The design foresees a registry that maps rule names to checkers. A new rule would then need only one entry and one function.

Figure~\ref{fig:design-dag} shows a complete proof as the verifier sees it.
\texttt{PRV089} proves the conjecture $\exists Z\,(p(Z) \land q(Z))$, that is, it refutes its negation, from the axioms $\exists Y\,p(Y)$ and $\forall X\,q(X)$.
The step \texttt{skA} Skolemizes \texttt{a1} with a fresh constant $\mathit{sK0}$, \texttt{instB} instantiates \texttt{a2} with this constant, \texttt{s} combines the two facts and \texttt{s2} generalizes $\mathit{sK0}$ back to an existential variable, which yields the conjecture itself.
The final step \texttt{bot} has the conjecture and its negation as parents.
Since they are complementary, it is decided without a prover, and the whole proof is verified by internal checks alone.
Each step is judged only by its parents. The graph and not the order in the file therefore determines what has to hold.

\label{subsec:design-rules}

Most families reduce to alpha-equivalence and structural matching.
The checks are deliberately strict. They accept only the shape that the rule prescribes. A conclusion that follows from the parents, but not by the cited rule, is therefore rejected.

The value of the rule checks shows in the invalid proofs of the benchmark.
In \texttt{PRV056}, a step instantiates
\[
  \forall X\,\forall Z\;\mathit{rel16}(X,Z,\mathit{sK0}(X,Z))
  \quad\text{to}\quad
  \mathit{rel16}(m_0,n_0,\mathit{sK0}(n_0,m_0)).
\]
Matching binds $X \mapsto m_0$ and $Z \mapsto n_0$. The third argument would therefore have to be $\mathit{sK0}(m_0,n_0)$, and the step is rejected.
The final \texttt{consequence} step of this proof would pass the prover, because the wrong instance still contradicts the negated conjecture.
Only the rule check of the intermediate step reveals the error.

\subsection{Checking Skolemization}
\label{subsec:design-skolem}

Skolemization cannot be validated by an entailment test (Section~\ref{subsec:normal-forms}), and the competition requires that it is checked internally. The checker therefore validates the following conditions, which together make the step satisfiability-preserving.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The step has exactly one parent, the status \texttt{esa} and declares exactly one new symbol of kind \texttt{skolem}.
  \item The Skolem term consists of the declared symbol applied to exactly the universal variables in whose scope the eliminated quantifier occurs.
  \item Replacing the quantifier and its variable by the Skolem term in the parent yields the conclusion up to alpha-equivalence.
  \item The symbol is fresh. It neither occurs in the problem nor was it introduced by an earlier step.
\end{enumerate}
The checker tracks the polarity while it traverses the parent.
An existential quantifier at a negative position is effectively universal and must not be Skolemized, and a quantifier below an equivalence occurs in both polarities and is rejected as well.
For example, \texttt{PRV004} Skolemizes $\forall X\,\forall Z\,\exists Y\,\forall W\; r(X,Z,Y,W)$ with the term $\mathit{sK0}(X,Z)$. The checker computes that $Y$ lies in the scope of $X$ and $Z$ but not of $W$, compares this with the annotation and checks the replaced formula against the conclusion.
Instantiation steps with \texttt{herbrand} symbols need no freshness condition, because an instance of a universal formula is entailed by it, and they are checked like other \texttt{thm} steps.

\subsection{Delegation, Verdicts and Soundness}
\label{subsec:design-atp}

\paragraph{Delegation to an external ATP.}

Steps without a rule-specific checker are validated by an external ATP, in line with the competition rules.
Only steps with status \texttt{thm} or \texttt{cth} are delegated, and the ATP receives exactly the premises of the step as axioms and its formula as conjecture, never the original conjecture or the rest of the proof.
The answers \texttt{Theorem}, \texttt{Unsatisfiable} and \texttt{ContradictoryAxioms} confirm the step, \texttt{CounterSatisfiable} and \texttt{Satisfiable} refute it and every other answer, a timeout, or a missing prover leaves it \emph{unresolved}.
For the junction rules, a failed shape check is not final, and the step is passed to the ATP, because their names are also used for unusual but valid variants. If the ATP does not confirm such a step, the original issue is reported.
Two sound shortcuts avoid prover calls: a final \texttt{consequence} step to \texttt{\$false} with complementary parents, and an \texttt{add\_hypothesis} step of the form $H \Rightarrow \varphi$ with parent $\varphi$.

\paragraph{Verdicts and soundness.}
\label{subsec:design-verdict}

Each step ends as \emph{valid}, \emph{invalid}, or \emph{unresolved}.
The verdict of the proof is \emph{failed} if any step is invalid, a global check fails, or the proof cannot be processed, \emph{uncertain} if any step is unresolved or the time limit is exceeded, and \emph{verified} otherwise (Table~\ref{tab:verdicts}).
The option \texttt{--binary-verdict} maps \emph{uncertain} to \emph{failed} for a two-valued output.

If the verdict is \emph{verified}, every condition of Section~\ref{subsec:verifier-def} has been checked, every \texttt{thm} step has been shown to follow from its parents by a rule check or the ATP, and every Skolemization step satisfies the conditions above.
The induction of Section~\ref{subsec:proof-objects-soundness} then shows that the conjecture follows from the axioms.
The argument assumes that the rule checkers are correct and that the ATP is sound, which Section~\ref{subsec:disc-tcb} discusses.

\paragraph{Resource control and alternatives.}
\label{subsec:design-resources}
\label{subsec:design-alternatives}

Each proof is checked in its own worker process, which is terminated when the limit is exceeded. A hanging ATP call or a crash therefore cannot affect the verdict of another proof.
Because all traversals of formulas are recursive and the benchmark contains deeply nested formulas, the worker uses a larger stack.

The main alternatives were considered and rejected for the ProoVer setting.
Sending every step to an ATP would make the verifier simpler, but slower, dependent on the ATP for every proof and unable to check \texttt{esa} steps at all.
A single generic entailment test instead of rule-local checkers could neither explain failures nor reject steps that follow by the wrong rule.
A binary verdict would score two points more on this benchmark, but loses more when the prover is missing or slow (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-ablation}).
