\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results, discusses what \TSTPy{} guarantees, examines how far it transfers to the proofs of an automated prover and lists its limitations.

\subsection{Interpretation of the Results}
\label{subsec:disc-results}

\TSTPy{} loses only two points on the benchmark and accepts no invalid proof, which matters more than the score, because a single wrong acceptance costs as much as ten correct ones.
This does not show that the verifier is complete.
The benchmark was available during development, and the invalid proofs contain the kinds of defects the checks were built for.
The Extra set found defects that the benchmark could not, and if the verifier loses points on unseen proofs, the fail-closed policy makes it lose them in the cheap direction.
The three-valued verdict costs two points here, but limits the loss when the prover is missing or slow.

The run times are not dominated by the prover, whose calls take \EvalAtpShare\,\% of the total time, but by the start-up of the interpreter.
The order ``cheap checks first'' is therefore mainly a matter of precision and independence from the prover.
It also explains why steps that Vampire cannot confirm within 10\,s, but that are trivial for an internal checker, do not endanger the verdict.

\subsection{Trusted Computing Base and Design Trade-offs}
\label{subsec:disc-tcb}

The soundness argument of Section~\ref{subsec:design-verdict} holds if the parser, the alpha-equivalence check, the rule checkers, the serialization of formulas for the prover, the verdict aggregation and the prover itself are correct.
A verifier that sends every step to an ATP has less own code, but it relies on the ATP, a saturation prover with a far larger code base, even for steps as simple as an instantiation.
The internal checks of \TSTPy{} replace the ATP for such steps. For most steps the trusted code is therefore the smaller one, about \EvalCheckerLines{} lines of checkers plus the parser and shared utilities, each of which can be read and tested rule by rule.
Measured against the de Bruijn criterion, this is a partial success.
The internal code is still an order of magnitude larger than an LCF kernel such as that of HOL Light \citep{Harrison2009HOLLight}, and every proof with a step that only the prover can decide also depends on Vampire.
The competition rules explicitly allow this compromise, since they permit external provers for rules without a fixed specification. A stricter verifier could still use external provers, but would require them to produce a certificate for every delegated step that a small trusted kernel checks, as GDV-LP does with LambdaPi \citep{Sutcliffe2025GDVLP}.
The correctness of the checkers is supported by tests, the mutation experiment and the benchmark, but not proved.

\paragraph{Design trade-offs.}
\label{subsec:disc-tradeoffs}

A proof that derives \texttt{\$false} in one step from the axioms and the negated conjecture, such as \texttt{TST043}, is delegated completely, and the verifier is then exactly as strong as the prover.
Because each rule has its own checker, every rule can be tested separately and every error can be traced to a single step.
Some checks still need information about the whole proof, such as whether a Skolem symbol is fresh, and these are done in the driver. The driver also still selects the checker for each step in a fixed chain of conditions instead of a modular registry.
Python favored readability and fast development, which was valuable for a thesis with many rules, and is fast enough for this task. Only for the very large proofs does the pure Python cost matter, and there mainly in the parser.
Process isolation makes timeouts and crashes harmless, but adds a start-up cost per proof and makes it harder to share state such as a cache of prover results.

\subsection{Transfer to Other Provers: A Case Study with Vampire}
\label{subsec:disc-transfer}

To see how much of the design survives when the proofs come from an automated prover, Vampire~5.0.1 was run in CASC mode for 30\,s on the 176 problem files of the ProoVer set, the deprecated pairs and the TST set.
It found 142 proofs (139 \emph{Theorem}, 3 \emph{ContradictoryAxioms}), and Table~\ref{tab:disc-transfer-a} shows what happens when they are given to \TSTPy{} unchanged.

No proof is verified, for three reasons.
\emph{Syntax}: Vampire records Skolem symbols as separate \texttt{introduced(definition,\dots)} formulas and cites input formulas as \texttt{file(\dots,unknown)}, which a normalizing front end could handle.
\emph{Vocabulary}: the 142 proofs use 22 inference names, the 176 reference proofs of these sets 39, and only \texttt{resolution} and \texttt{negated\_conjecture} occur in both, with different meanings. Most of the 22\,153 inferences belong to clausification and splitting, for example \texttt{cnf\_transformation} and \texttt{avatar\_*}.
\emph{Status}: in Vampire's output only \texttt{negated\_conjecture} and \texttt{skolemisation} carry a status. Under the fail-closed policy no other step may therefore be delegated.

The first experiment mostly measures format differences.
To test whether the \emph{checks} carry over, the 5\,141 Skolemization steps of the 176 reference proofs, at most ten per proof and mutation kind, were taken, mutants with a known defect were derived and the internal Skolem check was compared with a \emph{Vampire entailment check} that asks Vampire whether the conclusion $C$ entails the parent $P$ (Table~\ref{tab:disc-transfer-b}).
This is the only entailment that holds for a correct step, and it is what a verifier built around a generic prover would test.

Defects that change the formula are found by both, but the defect specific to Skolemization, a missing dependency, is found by the Vampire entailment check in none of the 25 cases, because a Skolem term with too few arguments makes $C$ stronger and $C \models P$ still holds.
Of the 35 flawed steps in the reference proofs, which include wrong annotations and \texttt{thm} instead of \texttt{esa}, the Vampire entailment check rejects only 11.
The one control step rejected by the Vampire entailment check is a Skolemization in the antecedent of an implication, which the internal check of the measured version also wrongly accepted and which the final version rejects.

A verifier that performs well on the competition set therefore does not automatically transfer to prover output. This is expected, because the benchmark is designed to test rule-level checks on a controlled vocabulary. TSTP fixes the syntax of derivations, but not a common set of inference rules and their semantics. Every prover therefore names and structures its steps in its own way \citep{TPTPQuickGuideDerivations}. One purpose of a competition such as ProoVer is to establish such a common ground, and a uniform rule vocabulary is what makes verifiers comparable in the first place. The gap to the output of Vampire is therefore a consequence of this early stage of standardization rather than a weakness of the benchmark or of the verifier.
The checkers are made for the rules of the benchmark, which resemble natural deduction and change a formula only a little at a time.
A proof from a prover looks different and consists mainly of clausification, splitting and saturation steps.
The invalid proofs of the benchmark contain deliberately chosen defects, whereas prover proofs would be wrong for other reasons.
Satisfiability-preserving steps such as Skolemization, definitions and \textsc{Avatar} splitting cannot be validated by an entailment test. A prover that checks equisatisfiability could validate them in principle, but delegating Vampire's own steps to Vampire would make the trusted base circular.
A direct extension would need a normalizing front end, native checkers for clausification and splitting and an argument for satisfiability-preserving steps, as certificate-based approaches provide \citep{Wetzler2014DRATtrim,Carcara2023}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:disc-limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scope.} Only FOF problems and the rule vocabulary of the ProoVer 2026 benchmarks are supported. Other rules are delegated or lead to abstention.
  \item \textbf{Weak rule checks.} \texttt{rewrite} steps omit their equation. Only conclusions equal to the parent are therefore accepted, which is sound but incomplete. Herbrand annotations are parsed but not checked separately.
  \item \textbf{Rules without semantics.} \texttt{trivial} with status \texttt{esa} and any other unknown rule with a status other than \texttt{thm} or \texttt{cth} lead to abstention.
  \item \textbf{Dispatch.} The checkers are selected by an \texttt{if}/\texttt{elif} chain instead of the registry of the design. A new rule therefore needs a new branch in the driver.
  \item \textbf{Prover calls.} The calls are sequential, and each may use the full limit of the proof. One hard step can therefore use up the time of the whole proof, as in \texttt{PRV099}.
  \item \textbf{Evidence.} The benchmark sets are large enough to find defects, but not to estimate rare failure rates, and the correctness of the checkers is supported by tests, not proved.
\end{itemize}
